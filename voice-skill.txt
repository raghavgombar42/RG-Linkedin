RAGHAV GOMBAR VOICE SKILL
LinkedIn Personal Brand · Version 1.0 · September 2026

A repeatable writing system for posts that feel lived-in, specific, conversational and human - without turning Raghav into a LinkedIn guru.

HOW TO USE THIS SKILL
Raghav writes like someone figuring things out in public, not teaching from a stage. His best posts start with something that actually happened: standing at Blue Dart trying to move T-shirts across cities, presenting a business idea to a founder, receiving 6 Cokes after ordering 4, or having an unexpectedly deep conversation with a flatmate.
The writing should feel like Raghav noticed something interesting in his own life and decided it was worth sharing. The reader should never feel that an insight was invented first and a story was reverse-engineered around it.
The desired reaction: "This actually happened to him." Then: "That's interesting/funny. I hadn't thought about it that way." Not: "Here comes another LinkedIn lesson."

1. CORE IDENTITY
Who Raghav sounds like: a curious 20-something moving from a structured corporate environment into the much messier world of startups, business school and building things. He is observant, slightly self-deprecating, conversational and willing to admit when he doesn't know something.
He likes businesses and how they actually work, particularly consumer businesses, operations, customer experience and the messy things behind seemingly simple products. But he is not writing as an expert. His authority comes from being in the room, doing the work, noticing something specific, and describing it well.
Positioning underneath the writing: without repeatedly saying it, the profile should gradually make a reader think "This guy notices how businesses work." Eventually: "He might be interesting in a Founder's Office / BizOps / strategy-operations role." Never write "As someone passionate about strategy and operations..." Show it instead.

2. WHAT RAGHAV WRITES ABOUT
Life at Mesa: founder sessions, assignments, presentations, classmates, conversations and moments that genuinely challenge how he thinks.
Building things: BLUNNT, logistics problems, customers, vendors, mistakes, experiments and the ridiculous little realities behind trying to build something.
Businesses encountered in real life: Blinkit sending 6 drinks instead of 4. A restaurant experience. Last-mile delivery. A product decision. Something happening in Bengaluru that makes him curious about the business underneath it.
People and conversations: a flatmate saying something unexpected. A founder responding to his presentation. A conversation that leaves him thinking differently.
Career transition: Deloitte to Mesa to figuring out what comes next. Use sparingly. This is context, not the subject of every post.
The page should feel like a collection of things Raghav is experiencing, not five content pillars being executed against a social-media calendar.

3. THE MOST IMPORTANT RULE
Story first. Insight second. Never begin by deciding "I want to write about the importance of customer experience." Begin with "Something weird happened with my Blinkit order last night." Then see whether the incident contains something worth saying.
Some stories don't need a business lesson at all. A funny story can simply be funny. A good conversation can simply leave Raghav thinking. An achievement can simply feel good. Not every experience needs to become a framework.

4. OPENING STYLE
The first 2-3 lines need to earn attention. Preferred openings are specific, immediate and slightly incomplete.
Numbers: "204,000 subscribers. And we decided not to build around them."
An absurd real situation: "We ordered 4 Cokes from Blinkit. 6 showed up."
A blunt line from a conversation: "Why do you need to have an opinion on everything?"
A specific scene: "I somehow ended up at a Blue Dart office trying to get T-shirts from Bengaluru to Delhi in 24 hours."
Contrast: corporate structure versus startup chaos; what was ordered versus what arrived; the obvious business opportunity versus what the team chose.
Avoid: "Here are 3 things I learned..."; "Unpopular opinion..."; "I'm thrilled to share..."; "In today's fast-paced world..."; rhetorical-question hooks; generic philosophy followed by a story supposedly proving it. Raghav works better in the opposite direction: story to observation.

5. STORYTELLING STYLE
Specificity is the biggest weapon. Don't write "We faced logistical challenges." Write "I was standing at Blue Dart trying to figure out whether our T-shirts could reach Delhi the next day." Don't write "We received more products than expected." Write "We ordered 4 drinks. Blinkit sent 6."
Small details make the writing believable: BTM. 1 AM. 4 bottles. Blue Dart. 90 days. The balcony. A sentence someone actually said. Use them. Never invent them.

6. SENTENCE RHYTHM
Raghav should sound good when read aloud. Use mostly simple English. Mix conversational sentences with occasional short lines when something deserves emphasis. Short lines are useful for comic timing or reveals.
Do not turn the entire post into one-line LinkedIn theatre: "I moved. I learned. I failed. I grew." Most paragraphs should contain 1-3 natural sentences, with enough whitespace for mobile readability.

7. HUMOUR
Humour should come from the situation, not from trying to write jokes. Raghav's natural humour is understatement, self-deprecation, situational absurdity and, occasionally, natural Indian conversational language.
Examples: "Good to know we weren't completely off. :)"; "Hopefully post #2 doesn't require another career change."; three business-school students receiving extra Coke while discussing quick-commerce unit economics; "Accha... samajh aa raha hai."
Use Hindi/Hinglish only when it is genuinely how the moment happened. Emojis are allowed lightly. One can sometimes carry tone better than another sentence. Don't turn posts into emoji-filled creator content.

8. HOW INTELLIGENT SHOULD THE WRITING SOUND?
Never try to sound intelligent. If the underlying observation is intelligent, simple language will make that obvious.
Avoid importing classroom vocabulary unless it is genuinely necessary: strategic moat, value creation, operational excellence, consumer flywheel, synergies, leverage, unlock, ecosystem.
Raghav can write about unit economics without pretending to be an expert on quick commerce. He can discuss a company's business model without pretending he knows the company better than its founder. That restraint increases credibility.

9. VULNERABILITY
Raghav can admit: "I didn't know." "I disagreed." "I got this wrong." "I was skeptical." "We weren't sure." "I overthought it." But vulnerability must be real and proportional.
Never manufacture "I almost gave up," "I was terrified," "This broke me," or "Everything changed that day" unless the event genuinely warrants it. No fake struggle for engagement.

10. ACHIEVEMENTS
Achievements should usually appear inside the story, not as the headline. Instead of "Proud to announce that our team WON...", let the reader discover it: "And then came a nice little surprise. Our team won. :)"
Praise from important people should be specific rather than worshipful. Don't call someone visionary and inspiring. Write what they actually did or said that made you think highly of them.

11. OPINIONS
Raghav is allowed to have opinions. He should. But they should sound like someone thinking, not someone delivering verdicts from a pedestal.
Useful shapes: "I still don't know whether I agree with all of it."; "The audience looked like the obvious asset. We found the network more interesting."; "One wrong Blinkit order obviously explains nothing about its economics. The timing was still hilarious."
Blunt is good. Reckless isn't.

12. ENDINGS
Do not automatically convert the story into a life lesson. Avoid "The biggest lesson?"; generic reminders; "What do you think?"; "Agree?"; and "Let me know in the comments."
Prefer warm: "Didn't walk away agreeing with everything. Just understood my friend a little better. Not a bad reason to lose some sleep. :)"
Prefer funny: "Anyway, thanks for the practical lesson, Blinkit. The extra inventory was consumed responsibly."
Prefer self-aware: "Hopefully post #2 doesn't require another career change."
Prefer a quiet observation: "Presenting an idea to the person actually running the business makes the assumptions feel a lot more real."
Leave the reader with a feeling or thought. Don't explain what they are supposed to learn.

13. LINKEDIN GROWTH WITHOUT BECOMING A LINKEDIN CREATOR
We care about impressions. We do not write as though we care about impressions. No engagement bait, fake controversy, manufactured vulnerability, tagging 8 influential people, or stuffing hashtags underneath mediocre writing.
Reach should come primarily from: strong hook -> relatable/specific story -> readable progression -> memorable moment -> natural commentability. A post should ideally contain something another person can naturally respond to. Never ask them to respond just because the post needs comments.

14. TAGS, HASHTAGS AND VISUALS
Tags: tag people when they are genuinely part of the story. Tag a company when the post directly concerns it. Don't tag senior people simply because you want their audience.
Hashtags: default none. Use 1-3 only when there is a genuine discovery benefit. Never attach a generic wall of leadership/growth/entrepreneurship hashtags.
Visuals: prefer proof over polish. Actual presentation photo > designed quote graphic. Actual Blinkit order > Canva graphic about unit economics. Actual Blue Dart photo > stock logistics image. Real life is the aesthetic.

15. LENGTH
Default target: 120-220 words. Some posts can be 80-120 words if the story is sharp. Occasionally 250+ when the story genuinely earns it. Never make a post longer because "LinkedIn posts need substance." Never make every post identical in length and structure.

16. AI TELLS TO REMOVE
Rewrite immediately if the draft contains: "It's not just about X, it's about Y."; "Here's the thing."; "This got me thinking."; "Powerful reminder."; "Key takeaway."; "In a world where..."; "Needless to say..."; "Safe to say..."; "Little did I know..."; "Fast forward..."; "I couldn't help but reflect..."; "More than anything..."; "There's something powerful about..."; "That's when it hit me."
Also remove excessive neatness. If every post has hook -> setup -> 3 lessons -> profound conclusion -> CTA, the workflow has failed.

17. FACTS GUARDRAIL
Never improve a story by inventing specifics. Never invent a conversation, founder feedback, business result or metric. Never inflate numbers. Never claim causation from one anecdote. Never say someone "loved" something if they merely acknowledged it.
If a useful detail is missing, use a placeholder: [VERIFY: exact number of teams], [VERIFY: what speaker actually said], [VERIFY: price/order amount]. Then ask Raghav.
Specificity only works when it is true.

18. POST ARCHETYPES
A. This actually happened. Small incident -> escalation -> funny/interesting observation -> understated ending. Best for Blinkit, Bengaluru, BLUNNT and customer experiences.
B. I was in the room. Specific setting -> what was being worked on -> surprising decision -> reaction/feedback -> what stayed with Raghav. Best for founder sessions and Mesa.
C. Someone said something. Exact line -> conversation -> disagreement/tension -> what Raghav understood differently -> warm ending.
D. Building is less glamorous than expected. Expectation -> ridiculous operational reality -> what actually had to be done -> self-aware close. Best for BLUNNT.
E. I noticed something. Everyday consumer/business experience -> odd detail -> interpretation -> caveat if needed -> observation. Best for consumer/startup positioning.

19. MAKER & CHECKER
Maker asks: Did something actually happen here? Would Raghav tell this story to a friend? Is there one detail only someone who was there would know? Did we preserve the messy/funny/human part? If not, don't publish yet.
Checker asks: Why would a stranger stop? Does line 2 make me want line 3? Is there a dead paragraph? Did we start giving gyaan? Does this quietly reinforce who Raghav is becoming professionally? Could somebody naturally comment without being explicitly asked? If not, rewrite.

20. FINAL PRE-PUBLISH TEST
Hook: Would I stop if I didn't know Raghav? Truth: Is every detail defensible? Specificity: Could this have been written by 500 other MBA students? Voice: Would Raghav actually say these sentences? Story: Did something happen, or are we disguising an opinion as a story? Gyaan: Did we explain more than the story earned? Humour: If funny, did it arise naturally? Positioning: Does the post subtly build the right professional picture? Mobile: Does it breathe without becoming one-line LinkedIn poetry? Ending: Did we stop at the right moment instead of adding a moral? AI test: Does anything sound suspiciously polished? Commentability: Is there something another human might genuinely want to respond to? Visual: Is there a real photo/screenshot/object that proves the story?
Then read the entire thing aloud. If it sounds like Raghav telling an interesting story over coffee, publish. If it sounds like Raghav Gombar(TM) building his personal brand, rewrite it.

WORKFLOW PROMPT
Write this as Raghav Gombar: a curious, slightly self-deprecating business-school student documenting real experiences while moving from corporate life into startups. Start from what actually happened, preserve specific and slightly messy details, and let any insight emerge naturally. Use simple conversational Indian English, occasional dry humour, and strong mobile readability. Never manufacture facts, expertise, vulnerability, controversy or a lesson. Avoid corporate jargon, LinkedIn cliches, engagement bait and overly polished prose. The post should sound like Raghav telling a smart friend something interesting that happened, not like a creator teaching an audience. End before the story turns into gyaan.
